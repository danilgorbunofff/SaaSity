# SaaSity — Product and Business Blueprint

> **Document purpose:** Define the business concept, customer value, core
> mechanics, commercial model, launch strategy, and operating principles for a
> new SaaSity project built from first principles.
>
> This is a product and business document. It intentionally contains no
> implementation architecture, database design, API design, or code-level plan.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

SaaSity is an explorable digital city where software products become buildings.
Founders receive a lasting place in the city and can purchase temporary
promotional campaigns when they want additional attention.

The city is not primarily a marketplace for speculative virtual land. It is a
visual SaaS discovery platform and promotional channel presented through a
memorable cyber-city interface.

The recommended model is:

1. **Permanent city membership is free or curated.**
2. **Paid products sell temporary attention, not permanent ownership.**
3. **Fixed prices are used while demand and traffic are developing.**
4. **Auctions are reserved for genuinely scarce, oversubscribed premium
   placements.**
5. **Campaign value is demonstrated through credible first-party performance
   metrics.**
6. **The city must be useful to visitors even when they have no intention of
   buying a placement.**

The central promise is:

> **Launch your SaaS inside an explorable startup city, earn a lasting place,
> and pay only when you want the city's attention.**

This model preserves the emotional appeal of owning a digital skyscraper while
creating a more rational, repeatable product for founders and marketers.

## 2. The Opportunity

### 2.1 The founder's problem

Early-stage SaaS founders struggle to earn attention. Existing channels are
crowded, expensive, short-lived, or difficult to measure:

- Social posts disappear quickly.
- Product directories often become static link collections.
- Launch platforms concentrate attention into a single day.
- Paid advertising requires expertise and sufficient conversion data.
- Sponsorships can be expensive and inaccessible to small founders.
- Communities may prohibit or discourage direct promotion.

Founders therefore need more than another place to submit a URL. They need:

- a presentable identity;
- credible discovery;
- a reason to share their presence;
- affordable promotional moments;
- clear evidence of the attention received;
- repeated opportunities to return for launches, updates, and milestones.

### 2.2 The visitor's problem

Visitors do not need another generic startup directory. They need an enjoyable
way to discover relevant products without browsing an endless list of cards.

SaaSity should help visitors:

- discover products by category or use case;
- understand what is new, popular, or actively launching;
- explore products visually;
- compare alternatives;
- save interesting products;
- follow updates from selected companies;
- return for changing events rather than seeing a static page once.

### 2.3 Why the city matters

The city is not decoration placed around a directory. It is the product's
memory and identity system.

A city provides:

- a spatial metaphor people immediately understand;
- visual differentiation from conventional directories;
- recognizable locations and districts;
- a natural representation of company growth and status;
- a stage for launches, events, sponsorships, and seasonal changes;
- screenshots and stories that founders want to share.

The city creates emotional value. Discovery, traffic, and analytics create
commercial value. SaaSity needs both.

## 3. Product Positioning

### 3.1 Category

SaaSity sits between:

- a curated SaaS directory;
- a visual discovery experience;
- a launch and promotion platform;
- a founder community;
- a niche advertising network.

It should not present itself as a virtual-land investment product. That framing
attracts speculation, creates legal and reputational risk, and weakens the
connection between price and marketing value.

### 3.2 Primary positioning

**For visitors:**

> Explore a living city of software products and discover what is launching,
> growing, and worth trying.

**For founders:**

> Give your SaaS a permanent home, then activate targeted promotion when you
> launch something important.

**For sponsors:**

> Reach a concentrated audience of SaaS builders and buyers through measurable,
> time-limited city campaigns.

### 3.3 What SaaSity is not

SaaSity is not:

- an NFT or token project;
- a promise of financial appreciation;
- a permanent-land resale market;
- a pay-to-win popularity ranking;
- a generic backlink farm;
- a one-day launch leaderboard;
- a platform that promises sales it cannot attribute.

These boundaries should remain explicit in product language and commercial
terms.

## 4. Target Participants

### 4.1 Primary paying customer

The initial paying customer is a United States-focused indie founder,
bootstrapped SaaS team, or early-stage startup with:

- a functioning product and landing page;
- a small but real promotional budget;
- an upcoming launch, feature release, or milestone;
- a desire for founder-community visibility;
- limited access to large advertising channels;
- willingness to experiment with a distinctive new channel.

The likely initial buyer is the founder, growth lead, or product marketer.

### 4.2 Secondary paying customers

Later customer groups may include:

- established SaaS companies seeking category awareness;
- agencies promoting client launches;
- accelerators presenting a portfolio cohort;
- developer-tool companies sponsoring a technical district;
- conferences or newsletters sponsoring a city event;
- investors or ecosystems presenting selected companies.

### 4.3 Visitors

The initial visitor audience should be intentionally narrow:

- SaaS founders;
- startup employees;
- developers;
- product managers;
- marketers;
- early adopters;
- buyers researching software tools.

A concentrated relevant audience is more valuable to advertisers than a large,
unqualified audience.

### 4.4 Curators and community contributors

Trusted curators may help:

- review submissions;
- organize districts;
- create collections;
- identify notable launches;
- prevent low-quality or deceptive listings;
- produce editorial content around city activity.

Human curation should initially be treated as a quality advantage, not an
operational failure.

## 5. The Core Product Model

### 5.1 City residency

Every approved SaaS receives a persistent building and profile in the city.
Residency represents membership, not ownership of a speculative asset.

A resident company should retain:

- a stable identity;
- a recognizable city location or district;
- a company profile;
- a link to its product;
- category and descriptive information;
- a history of meaningful milestones or past campaigns.

Basic residency should be free during the initial growth stage. Charging every
company merely to exist would make the city harder to populate and would
prioritize revenue before visitor usefulness.

### 5.2 Curated admission

Free should not mean automatic.

Submissions should satisfy basic quality standards:

- the product is accessible and understandable;
- the destination is safe and functional;
- the description is not misleading;
- the company fits an available district;
- the listing is not spam, impersonation, or a trivial duplicate;
- required disclosures are present.

The purpose of review is to protect visitor trust and the meaning of city
residency.

### 5.3 Districts

The city should be organized around understandable product categories, such as:

- AI and automation;
- developer tools;
- marketing and sales;
- finance and operations;
- design and creative work;
- productivity and collaboration;
- commerce;
- analytics and data;
- security;
- human resources.

Districts help visitors navigate and give promotional products contextual
relevance. A strong position inside the correct district may be more valuable
than a visually central position seen by an irrelevant audience.

District boundaries can evolve as the catalog grows. Empty districts should not
be created merely to make the city look large.

### 5.4 Building identity

The building should communicate useful product identity rather than serving as
an arbitrary purchasable skin.

Potential signals include:

- product category;
- company maturity;
- active launch status;
- verified milestones;
- community recognition;
- current campaign status.

Visual size must not imply financial success unless the underlying rule is
clear. Founders should not be encouraged to make unsupported growth or revenue
claims for a larger building.

### 5.5 Company profile

Every building should lead to a useful product profile containing:

- company name and concise description;
- target user or use case;
- category and tags;
- product website;
- current launch or announcement;
- verified social links;
- city activity and campaign history;
- platform-owned engagement metrics where appropriate.

The profile transforms a visual object into a discovery destination.

## 6. The Visitor Experience

### 6.1 First visit

A first-time visitor should quickly understand:

1. this is a city of real software products;
2. buildings can be explored;
3. highlighted buildings represent current activity;
4. useful search and category navigation are available;
5. the city changes over time.

The experience should not require understanding auctions, leases, or virtual
land before a visitor can discover a product.

### 6.2 Exploration modes

Different visitors will want different levels of immersion. SaaSity should
support several complementary modes:

#### Free exploration

The visitor moves through the city, selects buildings, and discovers products
organically.

#### Guided discovery

The platform provides tours such as:

- new this week;
- fastest-rising developer tools;
- founder favorites;
- products for solo businesses;
- current launches;
- staff selections.

#### Search and filtering

Visitors can search directly by:

- product name;
- use case;
- category;
- target customer;
- pricing model;
- current promotional status.

#### Event mode

During launches or sponsored events, the city changes temporarily and directs
attention toward relevant participants.

### 6.3 Reasons to return

The city cannot depend on visual novelty alone. Repeat visits should be driven
by:

- weekly launches;
- district rankings based on genuine engagement;
- editorial collections;
- product updates;
- city events;
- seasonal campaigns;
- followed-company notifications;
- saved-product updates;
- founder stories and measurable case studies.

### 6.4 Rankings and discovery integrity

Paid placement must be visibly labeled and separated from organic recognition.

Organic rankings should not be determined solely by raw clicks because that
encourages manipulation and rewards existing popularity. Rankings may consider:

- unique visitor engagement;
- saves;
- profile depth;
- recency;
- curator input;
- quality signals;
- suspicious-activity filtering.

The exact formula may remain private, but the principles should be public.

## 7. The Founder Experience

### 7.1 Joining the city

A founder submits a real product, selects the most appropriate district, and
provides a concise description. After approval, the product receives a
persistent building and profile.

The founder should immediately receive:

- a direct city link;
- a shareable visual;
- clear profile-completion guidance;
- an explanation of organic discovery;
- an explanation of optional paid promotion;
- transparent rules about metrics and ranking.

### 7.2 Living in the city

A founder can maintain the company profile and record meaningful events:

- initial launch;
- major feature release;
- pricing change;
- milestone;
- rebrand;
- acquisition;
- shutdown.

The city should represent an evolving ecosystem rather than freeze every
company at submission time.

### 7.3 Purchasing attention

When the founder has something worth promoting, they select a campaign rather
than purchasing land.

The campaign clearly states:

- what placement is included;
- when it begins and ends;
- which audience or district it targets;
- which distribution channels are included;
- what metrics will be reported;
- what is not guaranteed.

Once purchased, the campaign receives its promised period and cannot be
interrupted by another buyer.

### 7.4 Campaign completion

At completion, the founder receives a concise report such as:

> 2,430 qualified city impressions  
> 138 profile visits  
> 61 outbound website clicks  
> 17 saves  
> Ranked third in Developer Tools during the campaign

SaaSity should report only metrics it owns or can verify. Signups, paid
conversions, and revenue should not be claimed unless the founder voluntarily
uses a reliable attribution mechanism.

### 7.5 Renewal and repetition

Campaigns are finite and purpose-driven. A founder returns when there is
something new to promote, rather than paying indefinitely to prevent eviction.

This reduces resentment and creates a healthier recurring relationship:

- permanent identity remains;
- temporary amplification expires;
- past campaign evidence remains available;
- future promotion can be purchased when justified.

## 8. Paid Promotion Products

### 8.1 Product principles

Every paid product should:

- guarantee clearly defined deliverables;
- have a fixed duration;
- distinguish paid visibility from organic recognition;
- provide platform-owned performance reporting;
- avoid promising business outcomes;
- remain understandable without knowledge of advertising terminology.

### 8.2 Initial offer structure

The following prices are hypotheses for early testing, not permanent truths.

| Product | Illustrative deliverables | Duration | Initial price hypothesis |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| City residency | Persistent profile and building after approval | Ongoing | Free |
| Launch boost | Building highlight, launch feed placement, share asset | 7 days | $29–$49 |
| District feature | Prominent district placement and category promotion | 14 days | $79–$149 |
| City spotlight | High-visibility city placement, newsletter and social feature | 7 days | $199–$399 |
| Core event | Exclusive premium event or citywide campaign | Event-based | Auction or custom price |
| District sponsorship | Branded district presence and editorial package | 30 days | Established after demand proof |

Prices should be increased only after demonstrating consistent demand and
credible delivery.

### 8.3 Launch boost

Designed for indie founders and small launches.

The founder receives:

- a temporary visual highlight;
- inclusion in the current launch feed;
- improved discovery during the campaign;
- a shareable campaign image;
- a performance report.

This is the low-risk entry product and should be simple enough to purchase
without a sales conversation.

### 8.4 District feature

Designed for products that need visibility among a relevant audience.

The founder receives:

- stronger placement within one district;
- inclusion in a district collection;
- category-specific promotion;
- a longer campaign window;
- a detailed performance report.

The value proposition is relevance, not merely a larger building.

### 8.5 City spotlight

Designed for important launches or established products.

The founder receives:

- high-visibility placement;
- editorial context explaining why the product matters;
- a newsletter feature;
- a social announcement;
- a defined period of homepage or city prominence;
- a campaign report.

Editorial work must remain authentic. Payment guarantees the placement and
format, not an artificially positive review.

### 8.6 Sponsorships and events

Larger packages may include:

- sponsorship of a specific district;
- an accelerator cohort showcase;
- a conference-themed city week;
- a Black Friday software event;
- an annual awards program;
- a citywide launch takeover.

These products should be introduced only after the audience and operational
capacity justify them.

## 9. The Role of Auctions

### 9.1 Why auctions should not control ordinary residency

Continuous auctions create excitement but make poor default advertising
products:

- buyers cannot predict how long exposure will last;
- outbidding creates frustration after payment;
- low bidding activity publicly exposes weak demand;
- speculative participants can displace relevant products;
- complex rules reduce trust;
- a founder may optimize for winning rather than campaign performance.

For these reasons, ordinary city residency and standard campaigns should not
depend on an auction.

### 9.2 When an auction is appropriate

An auction is justified when:

- the inventory is genuinely unique;
- several qualified buyers have expressed interest;
- the promotional period is guaranteed after the auction;
- all bidders understand the deliverables;
- losing bidders are not charged;
- the event itself adds entertainment or publicity.

Suitable examples include:

- the weekly Core spotlight;
- an exclusive launch-day city takeover;
- a conference-week sponsorship;
- a high-attention seasonal event;
- a category sponsorship with multiple interested buyers.

### 9.3 Recommended auction experience

The auction determines who receives a future guaranteed campaign. It does not
interrupt an active campaign.

A successful bidder receives:

- a fixed start and end time;
- an uninterruptible promotional term;
- predefined campaign deliverables;
- transparent payment terms;
- the same reporting standards as fixed-price campaigns.

### 9.4 Fixed price versus auction decision rule

Use a fixed price when:

- demand is uncertain;
- inventory is not oversubscribed;
- simplicity improves conversion;
- the product is still being validated.

Use an auction when:

- there is verified excess demand;
- the inventory cannot be duplicated;
- competitive bidding creates positive attention;
- a price cannot be responsibly determined in advance.

An auction should reveal strong demand, not attempt to manufacture it.

## 10. Analytics and Proof of Value

### 10.1 Why measurement is central

The city's visual experience attracts attention, but measurement turns that
attention into a repeatable commercial product.

Without credible metrics, founders purchase novelty. With credible metrics,
they can evaluate SaaSity as a marketing channel.

### 10.2 Public metrics

Public information should establish platform credibility without embarrassing
individual companies or exposing commercially sensitive results.

Appropriate public metrics include:

- total monthly city visitors;
- total product discoveries;
- aggregate outbound clicks;
- active residents;
- geographic audience distribution;
- broad professional or interest profile;
- aggregate campaign benchmarks once sample size is sufficient.

Public claims should always define the measurement period and methodology.

### 10.3 Private campaign metrics

Campaign customers may receive:

- qualified impressions;
- unique profile visits;
- outbound clicks;
- click-through rate;
- saves or follows;
- district ranking during the campaign;
- traffic source breakdown;
- campaign-period comparisons.

### 10.4 Conversion attribution

SaaSity should not automatically claim responsibility for:

- account registrations;
- trial activations;
- subscriptions;
- revenue;
- fundraising;
- company growth.

These outcomes may be reported only when:

- the founder explicitly opts in;
- the attribution method is documented;
- privacy obligations are satisfied;
- the claim can be independently supported.

Founder testimonials should clearly distinguish measured results from personal
interpretation.

### 10.5 Fraud and metric integrity

Metrics lose all value if participants can manipulate them.

Operating policy should address:

- repeated self-clicking;
- automated traffic;
- click exchanges;
- misleading calls to action;
- purchased engagement;
- duplicate identities;
- hidden redirects.

Suspicious traffic should be excluded from reporting. Material enforcement
actions should be explainable to affected customers.

## 11. Pricing Strategy

### 11.1 Early-stage pricing principle

Before SaaSity has demonstrated traffic quality, buyers are purchasing novelty,
community participation, and experimentation. Prices should reflect that
reality.

High prices do not create prestige by themselves. Prestige emerges from:

- audience quality;
- selective admission;
- visible resident quality;
- repeated success stories;
- limited premium inventory;
- trusted editorial judgment.

### 11.2 Price discovery

Initial prices should be tested through:

- founder interviews;
- founding-cohort offers;
- measured conversion rates;
- repeat purchase behavior;
- campaign sell-through;
- customer-reported value;
- comparison with relevant sponsorship and directory products.

The strongest willingness-to-pay signal is a repeat purchase after the founder
has seen the first campaign report.

### 11.3 Founding cohort

The first residents should receive favorable terms in exchange for:

- honest feedback;
- permission to use measured results in case studies;
- complete and high-quality profiles;
- participation in launch activity;
- clear disclosure that they are founding residents.

Founding pricing should have an explicit end condition. Permanent unlimited
discounts can distort later economics.

### 11.4 Conditions for raising prices

Raise prices only when several of the following are true:

- campaigns frequently sell out;
- customers return;
- traffic quality remains stable;
- qualified click volume supports the increase;
- customers report meaningful outcomes;
- premium inventory has multiple interested buyers;
- delivery costs and editorial effort justify the price.

### 11.5 What not to price

Avoid charging for:

- fear-based protection from eviction;
- vague promises of exposure;
- unexplained visual size;
- unverified prestige;
- permanent ownership claims;
- access to metrics that should be standard campaign reporting.

## 12. Supply, Demand, and the Empty-City Problem

### 12.1 The marketplace challenge

SaaSity has two connected cold-start problems:

- visitors will not return to an empty or low-quality city;
- founders will not pay without visitors.

The product must first build valuable supply, then prove relevant demand, and
only then scale monetization.

### 12.2 Founding residents

Before broad public launch, recruit a curated cohort of approximately 20–40
credible products across a limited number of active districts.

Selection should favor:

- functioning products;
- diverse visual identities;
- founders willing to participate;
- products relevant to the initial audience;
- companies with real announcements during launch.

A dense small city is more convincing than a large empty city.

### 12.3 Controlled geography

The visible city should grow with occupancy.

Instead of presenting hundreds of empty plots:

- open a small number of districts;
- make active areas feel complete;
- unlock new areas when demand supports them;
- use expansion as a community event.

Scarcity should result from quality control and staged growth, not artificial
claims that unusable land is rare.

### 12.4 Demand generation

Initial visitor demand may be built through:

- founder-led launch stories;
- weekly curated product collections;
- social distribution;
- partnerships with newsletters;
- accelerator and community collaborations;
- useful category pages;
- editorial comparisons and discovery guides;
- shareable city updates.

The visual city earns the first click. Useful discovery earns the return visit.

## 13. Distribution and Growth Loops

### 13.1 Resident sharing loop

Every accepted founder receives a high-quality shareable city asset.

The loop is:

1. founder joins the city;
2. founder shares the building;
3. the founder's audience visits;
4. visitors discover adjacent products;
5. some visitors submit their own products;
6. city quality and awareness increase.

This loop depends on the asset being genuinely attractive and the city link
providing value beyond a static screenshot.

### 13.2 Launch campaign loop

1. A resident starts a campaign.
2. SaaSity creates a visible city event.
3. The founder and platform distribute the event.
4. Visitors engage with the launch and neighboring products.
5. Results become a campaign report.
6. Strong results produce case studies and repeat purchases.

### 13.3 Discovery loop

1. More quality residents improve search and collections.
2. Better discovery increases repeat visitors.
3. More relevant visitors improve campaign value.
4. Better campaign value attracts stronger residents.
5. Stronger residents further improve discovery.

This is the most important long-term loop because it does not rely entirely on
paid acquisition.

### 13.4 Reputation badge

A resident badge may be offered, but it should not be treated as valuable social
proof before SaaSity has earned a reputation.

Initially, the badge is primarily:

- a navigation link to the city profile;
- a visual identity asset;
- an optional participation signal.

Later, it may become a meaningful trust or recognition marker if admission and
quality standards remain strong.

### 13.5 Editorial and search distribution

The city experience should be complemented by indexable, useful editorial
surfaces:

- district guides;
- product comparisons;
- launch summaries;
- founder interviews;
- curated collections;
- trend reports based on aggregate city activity.

These surfaces should help users make decisions, not exist merely to generate
search traffic.

## 14. Trust, Quality, and Marketplace Rules

### 14.1 Admission integrity

Paid promotion must not automatically guarantee permanent admission. A product
must meet city standards before it can purchase attention.

### 14.2 Clear commercial labeling

Visitors should always be able to distinguish:

- organic recommendations;
- paid campaigns;
- sponsors;
- community rankings;
- editorial selections.

### 14.3 Founder identity and product claims

SaaSity should require accurate representation and reserve the right to request
verification for:

- company identity;
- official product links;
- performance claims;
- awards;
- customer counts;
- revenue or growth claims.

### 14.4 Inactive and closed companies

A living city needs an honest policy for companies that close or disappear.

Possible states include:

- active;
- paused;
- acquired;
- sunset;
- archived.

Closed companies may remain in a historical archive when educationally useful,
but they should not appear as active recommendations.

### 14.5 Disputes and refunds

Commercial terms should define:

- campaign cancellation windows;
- missed-deliverable remedies;
- treatment of platform outages;
- rejection of misleading campaign material;
- removal for policy violations;
- refund eligibility;
- what happens when the customer's destination becomes unavailable.

SaaSity should guarantee its own deliverables, not visitor behavior.

## 15. What Should Be Avoided

### 15.1 Permanent paid land ownership

Permanent paid ownership creates:

- stale inventory;
- abandoned links;
- speculative behavior;
- pricing disconnected from current audience value;
- difficulty evolving the city;
- pressure to describe virtual space as an appreciating asset.

Persistent residency provides the emotional benefit without creating a resale
market.

### 15.2 Continuous outbidding

Allowing a competitor to interrupt an active paid campaign makes budgeting and
planning impossible. Competition should occur before a guaranteed campaign,
not during it.

### 15.3 Plot subleasing and resale

A secondary market would introduce payment, dispute, speculation, and brand
quality problems before solving a meaningful customer need. It should not be
part of the initial model.

### 15.4 Universal public campaign results

Publishing weak per-company results may embarrass customers and damage sales.
Detailed campaign analytics should be private by default. Public case studies
should require permission and enough context to be honest.

### 15.5 Unproven premium pricing

A high central-tower price cannot be justified solely by visual prominence.
Premium pricing must be supported by audience reach, campaign delivery, and
excess demand.

### 15.6 Excessive gamification

Countdowns, scarcity, and competition can make campaigns entertaining, but
should not obscure:

- price;
- duration;
- deliverables;
- cancellation rules;
- performance limitations.

Urgency should reflect real inventory, not manufactured pressure.

## 16. Example Participant Journeys

### 16.1 Indie founder joining for free

Maya operates a small invoicing SaaS. She submits the product to the Finance and
Operations district. After approval, it receives a permanent building and
profile.

Maya shares the city view with her audience. Visitors can inspect her building,
read the product summary, save it, or visit the website. She is not required to
buy a campaign to remain listed.

Two months later, Maya launches automated payment reminders. She purchases a
$39 launch boost for seven days. Her building receives a visible launch state,
appears in the launch feed, and receives a final campaign report.

### 16.2 Growth lead purchasing a district feature

Daniel markets a developer-observability product. Before purchasing, he sees
aggregate audience information and benchmark ranges for Developer Tools
campaigns.

He buys a 14-day district feature for a new product release. The package
guarantees category placement, a district collection entry, and a performance
report. The campaign cannot be interrupted by another buyer.

Daniel later decides whether to repeat based on qualified profile visits and
outbound clicks—not on the visual size of the building alone.

### 16.3 Premium launch with competing buyers

Three AI products want the exclusive Core spotlight during a major industry
conference. SaaSity confirms sufficient interest and schedules an auction.

Each bidder sees the exact seven-day campaign package before bidding. The winner
receives the guaranteed period; losing bidders pay nothing and may purchase
standard district campaigns.

The active Core campaign cannot be displaced after it begins.

### 16.4 Visitor discovering a product

Alex arrives through a founder's shared building. Instead of leaving after
viewing one company, Alex opens a guided tour of new finance tools, compares
three profiles, saves two, and follows one company.

The city therefore creates value for the referring founder while also
supporting broader discovery.

### 16.5 Company shutting down

A resident founder marks the product as sunset. The building no longer appears
as an active recommendation. Its profile may remain in a historical archive
with a clear closure date.

The city stays trustworthy rather than becoming a collection of broken links.

## 17. Launch Strategy

### 17.1 Stage 1 — Validate the problem

Before building the complete experience, confirm that founders value:

- permanent visual residency;
- curated association with other products;
- affordable launch promotion;
- performance reporting;
- city-based share assets.

Interview both potential paying founders and potential visitors. Compliments
about the visual idea are not sufficient evidence of willingness to pay or
return.

### 17.2 Stage 2 — Build the founding city

Recruit a curated cohort and open only enough districts to make the city feel
active.

Success at this stage means:

- residents complete quality profiles;
- founders voluntarily share their presence;
- visitors explore more than one product;
- broken or misleading listings remain rare.

### 17.3 Stage 3 — Validate discovery

Before emphasizing monetization, determine whether visitors:

- understand the city;
- discover relevant products;
- use search and districts;
- save or revisit products;
- return for new activity.

If the city is visually impressive but produces shallow visits, the discovery
experience must be corrected before selling expensive campaigns.

### 17.4 Stage 4 — Test paid boosts

Offer a limited number of low-cost launch boosts to residents.

Evaluate:

- purchase conversion;
- campaign delivery;
- customer satisfaction;
- performance-report credibility;
- repeat purchase intent;
- operational workload.

### 17.5 Stage 5 — Expand premium products

Introduce district features and spotlights only after the entry campaign is
understood.

Auctions should remain disabled until premium inventory has more qualified
buyers than available slots.

### 17.6 Stage 6 — Establish recurring programming

Create a predictable rhythm:

- weekly launches;
- monthly district features;
- seasonal events;
- editorial collections;
- periodic city expansions.

Recurring programming gives visitors and founders a reason to plan around the
platform.

## 18. Success Metrics and Decision Gates

### 18.1 Supply quality

Measure:

- approved active residents;
- profile completion;
- active versus broken destinations;
- district density;
- submission approval rate;
- curator workload.

### 18.2 Visitor value

Measure:

- products viewed per visit;
- search and district usage;
- saves or follows;
- outbound product discovery;
- repeat visitor rate;
- guided-tour completion;
- time to first meaningful product interaction.

Time on site alone is not sufficient; visitors may spend time struggling with
navigation.

### 18.3 Founder value

Measure:

- resident share rate;
- organic profile engagement;
- campaign purchase rate;
- campaign completion satisfaction;
- repeat campaign purchase;
- founder referrals;
- voluntary case-study participation.

### 18.4 Commercial health

Measure:

- campaign sell-through;
- average campaign revenue;
- repeat revenue;
- refund rate;
- delivery cost;
- editorial and moderation cost;
- revenue per qualified visitor;
- percentage of revenue concentrated in a few buyers.

### 18.5 Integrity metrics

Measure:

- fraudulent traffic removed;
- misleading listings rejected;
- policy violations;
- broken-link rate;
- paid-placement labeling comprehension;
- campaign reporting disputes.

### 18.6 Key decision gates

Do not progress merely because a calendar milestone has arrived.

#### Gate A — City usefulness

Proceed to meaningful monetization only if visitors discover multiple relevant
products and show evidence of returning.

#### Gate B — Campaign value

Raise campaign prices only if customers understand reports, express
satisfaction, and begin to repurchase.

#### Gate C — Premium scarcity

Introduce auctions only when multiple qualified buyers repeatedly compete for
the same limited campaign.

#### Gate D — City expansion

Open new districts only when existing areas are sufficiently active and the new
category has credible supply.

## 19. Risks and Responses

### 19.1 Novelty fades

**Risk:** Visitors admire the city once and never return.

**Response:** Build recurring launches, useful search, editorial programming,
following, saving, and changing events. Treat the visual experience as an
acquisition advantage, not the full retention model.

### 19.2 The city becomes a pay-to-win directory

**Risk:** Paid companies dominate every useful surface.

**Response:** Preserve organic discovery, label sponsorships, cap promotional
inventory, and keep editorial selection independent.

### 19.3 Weak traffic makes campaigns unattractive

**Risk:** Early founders receive too little measurable value.

**Response:** Keep prices low, bundle platform distribution, recruit a relevant
audience, avoid exaggerated claims, and delay premium products.

### 19.4 Empty-city perception

**Risk:** Visible empty space signals failure.

**Response:** Launch compact, open districts gradually, and prioritize density
over nominal scale.

### 19.5 Low-quality submissions

**Risk:** Free residency attracts spam and weak products.

**Response:** Use curated approval, product standards, verified links, and
ongoing maintenance policies.

### 19.6 Visual complexity harms usability

**Risk:** The city is impressive but difficult to navigate, especially on
mobile or with assistive technology.

**Response:** Treat search, lists, guided tours, and accessible alternatives as
core product modes rather than secondary fallbacks.

### 19.7 Metrics are misunderstood

**Risk:** Founders interpret impressions as guaranteed customers.

**Response:** Define every metric, explain attribution limits, report only
verified data, and separate platform activity from customer outcomes.

### 19.8 Operational costs grow too quickly

**Risk:** Curation, editorial work, support, and campaign delivery make
low-priced products unprofitable.

**Response:** Measure delivery cost, standardize packages, limit inventory, and
increase prices only when customer value and demand support it.

## 20. Open Hypotheses

The following ideas should be tested rather than treated as facts:

1. Founders will value permanent visual residency enough to share it.
2. Visitors will use a spatial interface for real product discovery.
3. District context improves promotional relevance.
4. A $29–$49 launch product is an acceptable first purchase.
5. Campaign customers will repurchase after receiving credible reports.
6. Editorial and social distribution materially improve campaign value.
7. Limited premium inventory will eventually create authentic auction demand.
8. The city can maintain quality while allowing free residency.
9. Visitors will return for scheduled launches and evolving city activity.
10. The visual identity will produce lower acquisition costs than a conventional
    directory.

Each hypothesis should have an observable test and a defined condition for
reconsidering the model.

## 21. Strategic Options for Later

These ideas may become valuable but should not define the initial product.

### 21.1 Buyer-oriented discovery tools

The city may develop richer comparison, shortlisting, and procurement features
for people actively evaluating software.

### 21.2 Verified company milestones

Residents may display verified achievements such as:

- launch anniversaries;
- major customer milestones;
- funding announcements;
- acquisitions;
- public certifications.

Verification and context are essential to prevent empty status signaling.

### 21.3 Cohort cities

Accelerators, communities, or conferences may sponsor temporary city areas for
a selected cohort while remaining connected to the main discovery ecosystem.

### 21.4 Market intelligence

Aggregate, privacy-safe city activity could support reports about product
categories, launch activity, and buyer interest. This should never compromise
individual customer data.

### 21.5 Community programming

Founder discussions, launch reviews, office hours, or community awards may
strengthen retention if they remain focused and high quality.

### 21.6 Performance-based commercial models

Cost-per-click, affiliate, or outcome-based pricing should be considered only
when traffic quality, fraud controls, attribution, and customer economics are
mature enough to support them.

## 22. Final Product Principles

Every future product decision should be tested against these principles:

1. **Discovery before monetization.** The city must be useful without a
   purchase.
2. **Membership before ownership.** Companies belong in the city; they do not
   speculate on land.
3. **Attention is temporary.** Paid promotion has explicit deliverables and a
   fixed term.
4. **Identity is durable.** A campaign can end without erasing the company.
5. **Auctions require real demand.** Competition is introduced only when
   scarcity already exists.
6. **Relevance beats visual centrality.** Correct audience context is more
   valuable than arbitrary coordinates.
7. **Proof must be credible.** Report what the platform can actually measure.
8. **Paid visibility must be labeled.** Commercial activity should not weaken
   visitor trust.
9. **Density beats empty scale.** Grow the visible city with the community.
10. **The city is a means, not the entire value.** Visual novelty attracts;
    useful discovery and measurable promotion retain.
11. **No guaranteed business outcomes.** SaaSity guarantees its deliverables,
    not signups or revenue.
12. **Quality compounds.** Better residents attract better visitors, which
    creates better campaign value and attracts still better residents.

## 23. Recommended Starting Definition

The first version of the new project should be understood in business terms as:

> A compact, curated city containing real SaaS products across a small number
> of districts. Every approved product receives a lasting profile and building.
> Visitors can explore, search, and follow current launches. Founders can
> purchase a simple, guaranteed seven-day launch boost with transparent
> first-party analytics. Premium placements and auctions remain unavailable
> until actual demand proves they are needed.

This starting definition is deliberately narrower than the complete vision. It
tests the essential relationship:

> **Can a memorable city create useful software discovery, and will founders
> pay to amplify meaningful moments within it?**

If the answer is yes, SaaSity can expand into premium campaigns, sponsorships,
events, and selective auctions. If the answer is no, adding more complex
ownership or bidding mechanics will not repair the underlying value problem.
